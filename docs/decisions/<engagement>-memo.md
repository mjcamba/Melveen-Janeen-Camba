# <Engagement> — recommendation

**Recommendation.** Plant 10 tomato beds, 20 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds this season.  This 60-bed plan is expected to generate $42,761.66 in seasonal profit, or $42,762 rounded to the nearest dollar.

## Why
Stop tomatoes at 10 beds because the eleventh bed loses money at the margin.  tomato revenue is $8,800 per bed.  The tenth tomato bed has a marginal cost of $8,248.59, so it adds profit.  Placing the eleventh bed reduces profit by about $590.72.

Plant carrots and mesclun to their crop caps because their final permitted beds still add profit. The model selects all 20 carrot beds and all 30 mesclun beds. If the caps could be expanded without changing other conditions, one additional carrot bed would add approximately $352.49 in profit, while one additional mesclun bed would add approximately $246.47.

Do not spend money expanding general land or temporary labor capacity under the current assumptions. The recommended plan uses 60 of 64 beds and requires approximately 3.16 temporary workers out of the four available. Both constraints are slack. The crop-specific caps—not total land or temporary labor—are what currently limit profitable expansion.

## The judgment call
The recommendation treats the model’s cost and price assumptions as the operating conditions for the season and deliberately leaves four beds unplanted. That is a judgment call because unused land can feel inefficient. However, the remaining feasible crop to expand is tomatoes, and the next tomato bed costs more to produce than it earns. The decision is therefore not to fill every available bed; it is to avoid planting beds that lower total profit. If the farm can obtain additional carrot or mesclun capacity, carrots should receive priority because the next carrot bed has the higher estimated incremental value.

## What would change my answer
The recommendation would change if tomato price, tomato labor requirements, labor rates, or crop-specific bed caps changed materially.

If the tomato price rose above approximately $9,390.72 per bed, or if the marginal cost of the eleventh tomato bed fell below $8,800, the farm should reconsider planting an eleventh tomato bed.

If the carrot cap increased, the farm should evaluate additional carrot beds first because the next bed is estimated to add about $352.49 in profit.

If the mesclun cap increased, additional mesclun beds would also be worth evaluating because the next bed is estimated to add about $246.47 in profit.

If temporary labor became more expensive, less available, or capped below the current requirement of approximately 3.16 workers, the mix could change because labor costs are central to the decision.

If the farm gained a new profitable crop option, the current unused four beds could become valuable even though additional tomatoes are not.
