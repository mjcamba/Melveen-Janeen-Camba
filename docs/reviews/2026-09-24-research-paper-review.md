---

Your brief is real, and the thing in it worth protecting is the hypothesis: lower-income households
in Hawaiʻi take a larger hit to food security, chronic-illness management and household finances after
a climate-related disruption than higher-income households do. That is a distributional claim with a
comparison group built in, and the nursing vantage point — you see the medication problems and the
interrupted care that a price index never records — is a reason for a reader to trust the framing.
Everything below is about turning that sentence into a paper that can be written in four pages.

**The question is still a topic.** "How environmental disruption becomes an economic and health
problem in Hawaiʻi" is the subject of a book. Your hypothesis is narrower than your question, so let
the hypothesis be the question. That means naming three things the brief currently leaves open:
which disruption — one event, with a date — which price moved, and which two groups you are
comparing. Your Method section lists categories of sources ("state and county food and gasoline
price data, consumer price and cost of living data, public-health reports…"); the paper needs one
named series for the price and one named measure for the health or access outcome, each with a
source you can cite. Does the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Honolulu-area CPI separate food and energy
for the months around your event? Start there.

**Two hypotheses are stacked, and only one is the paper.** "Disruptions raise food and fuel costs" is
close to certain and is your premise; "lower-income households are hurt more" is the claim with
something at stake. Your falsification sentence repeats both. Keep the second, and let the first
become one paragraph of evidence.

**The brief names no economics, and the economics is already in it.** Hawaiʻi imports its food and
fuel, so a shipping disruption is a supply shock to a small island economy; food and fuel are
necessities, so demand for them barely falls when the price rises; and lower-income households spend
a larger share of income on them, which is exactly why the same price increase lands harder on them.
Price elasticity of demand for necessities, and budget share, are the two concepts your hypothesis
rests on. Name them in the brief and the paper has its framework.

**Who acts on the answer?** Recommendation is the heaviest criterion and the brief has no
decision-maker. Is this for a county emergency-management office deciding what to pre-position, the
Department of Health deciding where to send outreach, or a health system's occupational-health
program deciding how it protects essential workers after an event? Pick one, and the results you
foreground follow from it.

**Housekeeping.** The brief still opens with "Status: Skeleton. Headings below are mine to fill in,"
and a stray `capabilities/economic-research/spec.md.` line sits inside the Method section.

**In order:**

1. Rewrite the question as the hypothesis: one event, one price, two groups.
2. Name the two concepts — elasticity of demand for necessities and budget share — in the brief.
3. Name the decision-maker.
4. Pick the price series and the health or access measure, and record the source and the date you
   pulled each in `data/`.
5. Clear the skeleton line and the stray path from the brief.
